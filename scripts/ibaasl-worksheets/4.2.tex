{\LARGE\bfseries\color{titleblue}Worksheet 4.2 --- Measures of Central Tendency and Spread}\\[2pt]
{\small Mathematics: Analysis \& Approaches SL (IB) \textperiodcentered{} Unit 4: Statistics and Probability}
\end{center}

\begin{ideabox}
Standard deviation, quartiles, and the outlier rule describe how spread out data is.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
  \item $\sigma=\sqrt{\dfrac{\sum(x-\bar x)^2}{n}}$.
  \item Outlier rule: below $Q_1-1.5\,IQR$ or above $Q_3+1.5\,IQR$.
  \item Adding a constant shifts the mean, not the SD; multiplying scales both.
\end{itemize}
\end{ideabox}

\sech{Worked Examples}

\begin{example}{ex0}{Example 1 --- Mean and median}
Find the mean and median of $6,6,11,14,23$.\soln
Mean $=\dfrac{60}{5}=12$; median (middle value) $=11$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} mean 12, median 11.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex1}{Example 2 --- Standard deviation}
Find the SD of $4,6,8,10,12$.\soln
Mean $=8$; deviations squared: $16,4,0,4,16$; sum $=40$; $\sigma=\sqrt{8}$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} $\sigma\approx2.83$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex2}{Example 3 --- Outlier check}
A data set has $Q_1=22$, $Q_3=38$. Is $65$ an outlier?\soln
$IQR=16$; upper fence $=38+24=62$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} yes, $65>62$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex3}{Example 4 --- Transformation}
A data set has mean 55, SD 6. Every value is tripled, then 4 is subtracted. Find the new mean and SD.\soln
Tripling: mean $165$, SD $18$; subtracting 4 changes only the mean.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} new mean $161$, SD $18$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex4}{Example 5 --- Lower outlier check}
A data set has $Q_1=30$, $Q_3=50$. Is $2$ an outlier?\soln
$IQR=20$; lower fence $=30-30=0$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} no, since $2>0$ (it's above the lower fence).
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex5}{Example 6 --- Finding a missing data value from the mean}
Five values have a mean of 14: $8,12,17,19,x$. Find $x$.\soln
Sum must be $5(14)=70$. $8+12+17+19=56$, so $x=70-56$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} $x=14$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex6}{Example 7 --- SD from a frequency table}
Find the SD of the data $2$(freq 2), $4$(freq 3), $6$(freq 1) (6 values total).\soln
Mean $=\dfrac{2(2)+4(3)+6(1)}{6}=\dfrac{4+12+6}{6}=\dfrac{22}{6}\approx3.67$.\\[3pt]
Deviations squared (weighted): $2(2-3.67)^2+3(4-3.67)^2+1(6-3.67)^2\approx2(2.79)+3(0.109)+1(5.43)\approx11.14$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} $\sigma=\sqrt{11.14/6}\approx1.36$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex7}{Example 8 --- Comparing spread using range vs IQR}
Data set E has range 40 but IQR 5; data set F has range 20 but IQR 12. Which is more consistent in its middle 50\%, and why might the ranges be misleading?\soln
IQR measures the middle 50\% spread, ignoring extreme values; E's small IQR (5) shows most data is tightly clustered despite one or two extreme outliers stretching the range.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} E is more consistent in its middle half; its large range is likely driven by outliers, which the IQR correctly ignores.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{ex8}{Example 9 --- Application --- effect of a scaling error}
A scale reads every mass 2 kg too high, and additionally every reading was mistakenly recorded in a unit requiring multiplication by 1.1. If the true mean is 60 kg and true SD is 4 kg, find the reported mean and SD (assume the $+2$ happened before the $\times1.1$).\soln
Add 2: mean $62$, SD unchanged $4$. Multiply by $1.1$: mean $62(1.1)=68.2$, SD $4(1.1)=4.4$.\\[3pt]\textbf{Conclusion:} reported mean $68.2$ kg, SD $4.4$ kg.
\end{example}
