11.2 Bisecting Segments and Angles
To bisect means to split exactly in half. This worksheet builds the perpendicular bisector of a segment and the bisector of an angle using compass and straightedge, then proves — not just states — the two equidistance theorems that make these constructions useful: every point on a segment's perpendicular bisector is equidistant from the segment's endpoints, and every point on an angle's bisector is equidistant from the angle's two sides. These constructions and theorems are the direct foundation for locating a triangle's circumcenter and incenter in the next lesson.

Examples

Example 1: Perpendicular bisector — full justified steps
Construct the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB.
Solution
Two equal-radius circles centered at A and B always cross at two points that lie on the perpendicular bisector.
  1. Open the compass to more than half of ABABAB (this is essential — see Example 8).
  2. Centered at AAA, draw an arc above and below AB‾\overline{AB}AB.
  3. Without changing the radius, center the compass at BBB and draw a second arc above and below, crossing the first arcs at two points.
  4. Draw the line through those two intersection points.
Justification: both intersection points are the same distance from AAA as from BBB (equal compass radius both times), so the line through them is exactly the perpendicular bisector — the set of all points equidistant from AAA and BBB.
Answer: the constructed line passes through the midpoint MMM of AB‾\overline{AB}AB, perpendicular to it.
Example 2: Midpoint distance
AB=12AB=12AB=12. After constructing the perpendicular bisector meeting AB‾\overline{AB}AB at MMM, find AMAMAM.
Solution
The perpendicular bisector meets the segment at its midpoint, splitting it into two equal halves:
AM=MB=122=6AM=MB=\dfrac{12}{2}=6AM=MB=212​=6.
Answer: AM=6AM=6AM=6.
Example 3: Angle bisector — full justified steps
Construct the bisector of ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC.
Solution
Equal-radius arcs from D and E cross at a point that must lie on the bisector, by symmetry.
  1. Centered at vertex BBB, draw an arc crossing both rays of the angle, at points DDD and EEE.
  2. Centered at DDD, draw an arc inside the angle (any radius, but the same radius for the next step).
  3. Without changing that radius, center the compass at EEE and draw a second arc, crossing the first.
  4. Draw the ray from BBB through that intersection point.
Justification: the intersection point is equidistant from DDD and EEE (equal radii), and BD=BEBD=BEBD=BE (step 1), so △BD(pt)≅△BE(pt)\triangle BD(\text{pt})\cong\triangle BE(\text{pt})△BD(pt)≅△BE(pt) by SSS, forcing the two half-angles to be congruent.
Answer: the constructed ray bisects ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC into two congruent angles.
Example 4: Splitting an angle
m∠ABC=80∘m\angle ABC=80^\circm∠ABC=80∘ is bisected. Find the measure of each resulting angle.
Solution
Bisecting splits the angle into two congruent halves:
80∘2=40∘\dfrac{80^\circ}{2}=40^\circ280∘​=40∘ each.
Answer: 40∘40^\circ40∘ and 40∘40^\circ40∘.
Example 5: Why the bisector meets the segment at exactly $90^\circ$
Explain, using the two arc-intersection points CCC and DDD from the perpendicular bisector construction, why the resulting line must be perpendicular to AB‾\overline{AB}AB (not just close to it).
Solution
By construction, CA=CBCA=CBCA=CB and DA=DBDA=DBDA=DB (each pair from an equal-radius arc), so ACBDACBDACBD is a kite (two pairs of adjacent congruent sides). A kite's diagonals are always perpendicular, and here one diagonal is AB‾\overline{AB}AB and the other is CD‾\overline{CD}CD — so CD‾⊥AB‾\overline{CD}\perp\overline{AB}CD⊥AB exactly, not approximately.
Answer: ACBDACBDACBD is a kite, and a kite's diagonals are perpendicular, so the constructed line meets AB‾\overline{AB}AB at exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Example 6: Proving the Perpendicular Bisector Theorem
Point PPP lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB, meeting it at MMM. Prove PA=PBPA=PBPA=PB.
Solution
Compare △PMA\triangle PMA△PMA and △PMB\triangle PMB△PMB:
AM=MBAM=MBAM=MB (definition of bisector — MMM is the midpoint).
∠PMA=∠PMB=90∘\angle PMA=\angle PMB=90^\circ∠PMA=∠PMB=90∘ (definition of perpendicular).
PM=PMPM=PMPM=PM (shared side).
By SAS, △PMA≅△PMB\triangle PMA\cong\triangle PMB△PMA≅△PMB, so their corresponding sides PAPAPA and PBPBPB are congruent.
Answer: PA=PBPA=PBPA=PB, proven by SAS congruence.
Example 7: Proving the Angle Bisector Theorem
Point QQQ lies on the bisector of ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC. Perpendiculars are dropped from QQQ to each side, meeting ray BABABA at RRR and ray BCBCBC at SSS. Prove QR=QSQR=QSQR=QS.
Solution
Compare △BQR\triangle BQR△BQR and △BQS\triangle BQS△BQS:
∠QBR=∠QBS\angle QBR=\angle QBS∠QBR=∠QBS (QQQ is on the bisector, so it splits ∠ABC\angle ABC∠ABC into two equal parts).
∠BRQ=∠BSQ=90∘\angle BRQ=\angle BSQ=90^\circ∠BRQ=∠BSQ=90∘ (given, perpendiculars).
BQ=BQBQ=BQBQ=BQ (shared side).
By AAS, △BQR≅△BQS\triangle BQR\cong\triangle BQS△BQR≅△BQS, so QR=QSQR=QSQR=QS.
Answer: QR=QSQR=QSQR=QS, proven by AAS congruence — QQQ is equidistant from both sides of the angle.
Example 8: Why the compass radius must exceed half of $AB$
Explain what goes wrong if the compass radius used for the perpendicular bisector arcs is less than half of ABABAB.
Solution
Each arc from AAA and from BBB has radius rrr. For the two arcs to intersect at all, the circles of radius rrr centered at AAA and BBB (distance ABABAB apart) must overlap, which requires 2r>AB2r>AB2r>AB, i.e. r>AB2r>\dfrac{AB}{2}r>2AB​. If r≤AB2r\le\dfrac{AB}{2}r≤2AB​, the two circles are too far apart (or just touching) and never cross at two distinct points, so the construction fails to locate the bisector.
Answer: the arcs would not intersect (or only touch at one point on AB‾\overline{AB}AB itself), so the perpendicular bisector could not be located.
Example 9: Algebra with an angle bisector
An angle of measure (4x)∘(4x)^\circ(4x)∘ is bisected into two angles each measuring (x+10)∘(x+10)^\circ(x+10)∘. Find xxx.

Each half equals half the whole angle:
4x=2(x+10)4x=2(x+10)4x=2(x+10)
4x=2x+204x=2x+204x=2x+20

Answer: x=10x=10x=10.

MMM is the midpoint of AB‾\overline{AB}AB. If AM=3x−1AM=3x-1AM=3x−1 and AB=2x+6AB=2x+6AB=2x+6, find xxx and the length of AB‾\overline{AB}AB.

Since MMM is the midpoint, AM=12ABAM=\dfrac12ABAM=21​AB:
3x−1=12(2x+6)3x-1=\dfrac12(2x+6)3x−1=21​(2x+6)

Then AB=2(2)+6=10AB=2(2)+6=10AB=2(2)+6=10, and AM=3(2)−1=5AM=3(2)-1=5AM=3(2)−1=5 (indeed half of 101010).

Example 11: Combining both constructions — a real-world locator
A new fire station must be built (a) equidistant from towns AAA and BBB, and (b) equidistant from two straight roads that meet at an angle at point CCC. Describe the two constructions needed and what determines the final location.
Solution
Condition (a) means the station lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB (Example 1) — every point there is equidistant from AAA and BBB.
Condition (b) means the station lies on the angle bisector of the angle formed by the two roads at CCC (Example 3) — every point there is equidistant from both roads.
Constructing both lines and finding where they cross gives the one location (if any) satisfying both conditions simultaneously.
Answer: construct the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB and the angle bisector at CCC; their intersection point is the required station location.
