4.1 Classifying Triangles & Angle Sum
Triangles are classified two independent ways. By sides: scalene (no two sides congruent), isosceles (at least two sides congruent), equilateral (all three sides congruent). By angles: acute (all three angles &lt;90^\circ), right (exactly one 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle), obtuse (exactly one angle &gt;90^\circ), equiangular (all three angles congruent, which forces equilateral). The Triangle Angle-Sum Theorem guarantees the interior angles of every triangle total 180∘180^\circ180∘; its corollary, the Exterior Angle Theorem, says an exterior angle equals the sum of the two non-adjacent (remote) interior angles. Because the angle sum is fixed at 180∘180^\circ180∘, a triangle can never have two right angles or two obtuse angles, and the acute angles of a right triangle must be complementary. This worksheet blends definitions, algebra, coordinate geometry, and short proofs.

Examples

Example 1: Classify by sides
A triangle has sides 888, 888, and 131313. Classify it by its sides.
Solution
Tick marks show the two congruent sides.
Compare all three side lengths: 888, 888, 131313.
Two of the three sides are congruent (8=88=88=8); the third is different.
This matches the definition of isosceles (not equilateral, since not all three match; not scalene, since two match).
Answer: Isosceles.

Example 5: Prove the Exterior Angle Theorem
Prove that an exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.
Solution
Extend a side of the triangle to form exterior ∠ACD\angle ACD∠ACD.
Let the triangle's interior angles be ∠A\angle A∠A, ∠B\angle B∠B, ∠C\angle C∠C, and extend side BC‾\overline{BC}BC through CCC to point DDD, forming exterior angle ∠ACD\angle ACD∠ACD.
By the Angle-Sum Theorem: ∠A+∠B+∠C=180∘\angle A+\angle B+\angle C=180^{\circ}∠A+∠B+∠C=180∘, so ∠A+∠B=180∘−∠C\angle A+\angle B=180^{\circ}-\angle C∠A+∠B=180∘−∠C.
Because ∠ACD\angle ACD∠ACD and ∠C\angle C∠C form a linear pair (they lie on straight line BDBDBD): ∠ACD+∠C=180∘\angle ACD+\angle C=180^{\circ}∠ACD+∠C=180∘, so ∠ACD=180∘−∠C\angle ACD=180^{\circ}-\angle C∠ACD=180∘−∠C.
Both expressions equal 180∘−∠C180^{\circ}-\angle C180∘−∠C, so ∠ACD=∠A+∠B\angle ACD=\angle A+\angle B∠ACD=∠A+∠B. ■\blacksquare■
Conclusion: the exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote (non-adjacent) interior angles.
Example 6: Apply the Exterior Angle Theorem

Solution
By the Exterior Angle Theorem, the exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles:
130=55+(other remote)130 = 55 + (\text{other remote})130=55+(other remote)
Other remote angle =130−55=75∘=130-55=75^{\circ}=130−55=75∘.
The adjacent interior angle is supplementary to the exterior angle (linear pair): 180−130=50∘180-130=50^{\circ}180−130=50∘.
Check all three interior angles: 55+75+50=18055+75+50=18055+75+50=180 ✓.
Answer: Other remote angle =75∘=75^{\circ}=75∘; adjacent interior angle =50∘=50^{\circ}=50∘.

Example 9: Why two obtuse angles is impossible
Explain why a triangle cannot have two obtuse angles.
Solution
Suppose, for contradiction, a triangle had two obtuse angles, each greater than 90∘90^{\circ}90∘.
Their sum alone would already exceed 90∘+90∘=180∘90^{\circ}+90^{\circ}=180^{\circ}90∘+90∘=180∘.
But the full angle sum of any triangle is exactly 180∘180^{\circ}180∘, and angle measures must be positive.
Adding a third positive angle would push the total above 180∘180^{\circ}180∘, contradicting the Angle-Sum Theorem.
Conclusion: a triangle can have at most one obtuse angle (and, by the same reasoning, at most one right angle).

∠B=180−130=50∘\angle B = 180-130=50^{\circ}∠B=180−130=50∘.
