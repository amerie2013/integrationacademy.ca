3.6 Transformations and Congruence
The modern definition of congruence is built entirely on rigid motions: two figures are congruent exactly when some sequence of translations, reflections, and rotations maps one perfectly onto the other. This is more powerful than just comparing measurements, because it explains why corresponding parts must match (CPCTC) and it works even when the mapping needs two or three steps chained together. This section proves congruence with explicit coordinates, contrasts congruence with dilation (which changes size and breaks the rigid-motion definition), and reverses the process to find the transformation sequence from two given triangles.

Examples

Example 1: Definition of congruence via rigid motions
What must be true for two figures to be congruent, in terms of transformations?
Solution
Two congruent triangles — one is a rigid-motion image of the other.
Two figures are congruent exactly when some sequence of rigid motions (translations, reflections, rotations) maps one exactly onto the other.
Because rigid motions preserve both distance and angle measure, congruent figures automatically have equal corresponding side lengths and equal corresponding angles.
Answer: A sequence of rigid motions maps one figure exactly onto the other.
Example 2: The three rigid motions
Name the three basic rigid motions, and state the one property they all share that makes congruence proofs possible.
Solution
The three basic rigid motions are translation, reflection, and rotation (compositions of these, like glide reflections, are also rigid motions).
The shared property: every one of them preserves distance (and therefore angle measure) — nothing stretches, shrinks, or distorts.
Answer: Translation, reflection, rotation; they all preserve distance.
Example 3: Proving congruence with coordinates
△ABC\triangle ABC△ABC has A(0,0)A(0,0)A(0,0), B(4,0)B(4,0)B(4,0), C(0,3)C(0,3)C(0,3). △DEF\triangle DEF△DEF has D(0,0)D(0,0)D(0,0), E(0,4)E(0,4)E(0,4), F(−3,0)F(-3,0)F(−3,0). Show a rigid motion maps △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC to △DEF\triangle DEF△DEF, proving they are congruent.
Solution
Rotating △ABC by 90° CCW about the origin lands exactly on △DEF.
Test the 90∘90^\circ90∘ CCW rule (x,y)→(−y,x)(x,y)\to(-y,x)(x,y)→(−y,x) on each vertex: A(0,0)→(0,0)=DA(0,0)\to(0,0)=DA(0,0)→(0,0)=D; B(4,0)→(0,4)=EB(4,0)\to(0,4)=EB(4,0)→(0,4)=E; C(0,3)→(−3,0)=FC(0,3)\to(-3,0)=FC(0,3)→(−3,0)=F.
Every vertex lands exactly on its target, so this single rotation maps △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC onto △DEF\triangle DEF△DEF.
Answer: A 90∘90^\circ90∘ CCW rotation about the origin proves △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF.
Example 4: CPCTC for sides
If a rigid motion maps △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC to △DEF\triangle DEF△DEF (so △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF), explain why AB‾≅DE‾\overline{AB} \cong \overline{DE}AB≅DE must be true.
Solution
The rigid motion sends A→DA\to DA→D and B→EB\to EB→E by definition of the correspondence, and rigid motions preserve distance.
So the distance from AAA to BBB must equal the distance from the image of AAA to the image of BBB, which is the distance from DDD to EEE.
Answer: AB‾≅DE‾\overline{AB} \cong \overline{DE}AB≅DE — this is exactly what "Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent" (CPCTC) means.
Example 5: CPCTC for angles
△ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF. Explain why ∠C≅∠F\angle C \cong \angle F∠C≅∠F must be true, using the same reasoning as Example 4.
Solution
Rigid motions preserve angle measure just as they preserve distance, and the correspondence sends C→FC \to FC→F (along with A→DA\to DA→D, B→EB\to EB→E).
So the angle at CCC (between the images of the two sides meeting there) must equal the angle at FFF.
Answer: ∠C≅∠F\angle C \cong \angle F∠C≅∠F (CPCTC).
Example 6: Why dilation breaks congruence
A triangle with sides 333, 444, 555 is dilated by scale factor 222, producing sides 666, 888, 101010. Is the image congruent to the original? Explain using the rigid-motion definition.
Solution
A dilation by a factor other than 111 multiplies every length by that factor — it is not distance-preserving, so it is not a rigid motion.
Since congruence requires a sequence of rigid motions, and dilation isn't one, the enlarged triangle is not congruent to the original — it is only similar (same shape, proportional sides).
Answer: Not congruent — the side lengths 3,4,53,4,53,4,5 vs. 6,8,106,8,106,8,10 are proportional but not equal.
Example 7: Congruence through a two-step composition
△PQR\triangle PQR△PQR has P(1,1)P(1,1)P(1,1), Q(4,1)Q(4,1)Q(4,1), R(1,3)R(1,3)R(1,3). Reflect over the xxx-axis, then translate by ⟨2,5⟩\langle2,5\rangle⟨2,5⟩. Verify the image triangle is congruent to △PQR\triangle PQR△PQR by checking that side PQPQPQ keeps the same length.
Solution
A composition of two rigid motions still preserves every length.
Original length: PQ=∣4−1∣=3PQ = |4-1| = 3PQ=∣4−1∣=3 (same yyy-coordinate).
Reflect: P→(1,−1)P\to(1,-1)P→(1,−1), Q→(4,−1)Q\to(4,-1)Q→(4,−1); translate by ⟨2,5⟩\langle2,5\rangle⟨2,5⟩: P′′=(3,4)P''=(3,4)P′′=(3,4), Q′′=(6,4)Q''=(6,4)Q′′=(6,4).
New length: P′′Q′′=∣6−3∣=3P''Q'' = |6-3| = 3P′′Q′′=∣6−3∣=3 — identical to the original.
Answer: PQ=P′′Q′′=3PQ = P''Q'' = 3PQ=P′′Q′′=3, confirming the composition preserved length, so the triangles are congruent.
Example 8: Congruent figures can have opposite orientation
Two triangles are congruent, but one requires a reflection (an odd number of reflections) to map onto the other, while the other could be reached by rotations and translations alone. Are both pairs still congruent?
Solution
Congruence only requires some sequence of rigid motions — reflections count just as much as translations and rotations.
A figure and its mirror image (like a left hand and a right hand shape) are still congruent to each other; they simply have opposite orientation.
Answer: Yes — congruence does not require matching orientation, only equal size and shape.
Example 9: Correspondence order matters
△ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF specifically means A↔DA\leftrightarrow DA↔D, B↔EB\leftrightarrow EB↔E, C↔FC\leftrightarrow FC↔F. If someone instead writes △ABC≅△EDF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle EDF△ABC≅△EDF for the same two triangles, does that claim the same correspondence?
Solution
No — the order of the letters names the correspondence: △ABC≅△EDF\triangle ABC\cong\triangle EDF△ABC≅△EDF claims A↔EA\leftrightarrow EA↔E, B↔DB\leftrightarrow DB↔D, C↔FC\leftrightarrow FC↔F, a completely different pairing of parts.
For a scalene triangle, only one correspondence (up to the triangle's own symmetry, usually none) will actually make every pair of sides and angles match — writing the letters in the wrong order states a false congruence, even if the two triangles happen to be congruent in general.
Answer: No — correspondence order is part of the meaning of a congruence statement, not just labeling.
Example 10: Reverse-engineer the transformation
△ABC\triangle ABC△ABC has A(2,1)A(2,1)A(2,1), B(6,1)B(6,1)B(6,1), C(2,4)C(2,4)C(2,4). Its image is A′(0,−3)A'(0,-3)A′(0,−3), B′(−4,−3)B'(-4,-3)B′(−4,−3), C′(0,0)C'(0,0)C′(0,0). Find a sequence of two rigid motions that maps △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC to △A′B′C′\triangle A'B'C'△A′B′C′.
Solution
Try a reflection over the yyy-axis first: A(2,1)→(−2,1)A(2,1)\to(-2,1)A(2,1)→(−2,1); B(6,1)→(−6,1)B(6,1)\to(-6,1)B(6,1)→(−6,1); C(2,4)→(−2,4)C(2,4)\to(-2,4)C(2,4)→(−2,4).
Find the translation needed to finish matching AAA: (−2,1)→(0,−3)(-2,1)\to(0,-3)(−2,1)→(0,−3) requires vector ⟨2,−4⟩\langle2,-4\rangle⟨2,−4⟩.
Check the same vector on BBB: (−6,1)+⟨2,−4⟩=(−4,−3)(-6,1)+\langle2,-4\rangle=(-4,-3)(−6,1)+⟨2,−4⟩=(−4,−3) — matches B′B'B′. ✓ Check on CCC: (−2,4)+⟨2,−4⟩=(0,0)(-2,4)+\langle2,-4\rangle=(0,0)(−2,4)+⟨2,−4⟩=(0,0) — matches C′C'C′. ✓
Answer: Reflect over the yyy-axis, then translate by ⟨2,−4⟩\langle2,-4\rangle⟨2,−4⟩ — since every vertex matches, △ABC≅△A′B′C′\triangle ABC \cong \triangle A'B'C'△ABC≅△A′B′C′.
