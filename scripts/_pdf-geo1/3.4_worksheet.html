3.4 Compositions of Transformations
A composition applies one rigid motion, then another, to build a single combined transformation — and the order in which you apply them can change the final image, which surprises many students used to addition being commutative. This section proves two elegant theorems (two reflections over parallel lines make a translation; two reflections over intersecting lines make a rotation through double the angle between them), defines the glide reflection precisely, and works both forward (apply a sequence) and backward (find the missing vector or angle from a known result).

Examples

Example 1: Order can matter
Translate (2,1)(2,1)(2,1) by ⟨3,0⟩\langle3,0\rangle⟨3,0⟩, then reflect over the yyy-axis. Separately, reflect (2,1)(2,1)(2,1) over the yyy-axis first, then translate by ⟨3,0⟩\langle3,0\rangle⟨3,0⟩. Compare the two results.
Solution
Two different orders give two different final points.
Translate-then-reflect: (2,1)+⟨3,0⟩=(5,1)(2,1)+\langle3,0\rangle=(5,1)(2,1)+⟨3,0⟩=(5,1); reflect over the yyy-axis: (−5,1)(-5,1)(−5,1).
Reflect-then-translate: reflect (2,1)(2,1)(2,1) over the yyy-axis first: (−2,1)(-2,1)(−2,1); then translate: (−2+3, 1)=(1,1)(-2+3,\,1)=(1,1)(−2+3,1)=(1,1).
Answer: (−5,1)(-5,1)(−5,1) vs. (1,1)(1,1)(1,1) — different points, so composition order is not generally commutative.
Example 2: A true glide reflection
A glide reflection translates a figure along a line, then reflects it over that same line. Apply "translate by ⟨4,0⟩\langle4,0\rangle⟨4,0⟩, then reflect over the xxx-axis" to the point (1,2)(1,2)(1,2).
Solution
Slide, then flip over the same line — like alternating footprints.
Translate: (1,2)+⟨4,0⟩=(5,2)(1,2)+\langle4,0\rangle=(5,2)(1,2)+⟨4,0⟩=(5,2).
Reflect over the xxx-axis: (5,2)→(5,−2)(5,2)\to(5,-2)(5,2)→(5,−2).
Answer: (5,−2)(5,-2)(5,−2) — this slide-then-flip pattern is exactly how alternating footprints are modeled in geometry.
Example 3: Two reflections over parallel lines make a translation
Reflect (x,y)(x,y)(x,y) over the line x=1x=1x=1, then reflect that image over the line x=4x=4x=4. Show the net result is a translation, and find the vector.
Solution
First reflection over x=1x=1x=1: x′=2(1)−x=2−xx' = 2(1)-x = 2-xx′=2(1)−x=2−x (yyy unchanged).
Second reflection over x=4x=4x=4: x′′=2(4)−x′=8−(2−x)=x+6x'' = 2(4)-x' = 8-(2-x) = x+6x′′=2(4)−x′=8−(2−x)=x+6 (yyy still unchanged).
Net effect: (x,y)→(x+6, y)(x,y)\to(x+6,\,y)(x,y)→(x+6,y) — a translation by ⟨6,0⟩\langle6,0\rangle⟨6,0⟩, and 6=2×(4−1)6 = 2\times(4-1)6=2×(4−1) is exactly twice the gap between the mirror lines.
Answer: The composition is a translation by ⟨6,0⟩\langle6,0\rangle⟨6,0⟩.
Example 4: Two reflections over intersecting lines make a rotation
Two lines intersect at the origin at an angle of 25∘25^\circ25∘ between them. A figure is reflected over the first line, then over the second. What single transformation is equivalent, and through what angle?
Solution
Theorem: reflecting over two intersecting lines is equivalent to a single rotation about their intersection point, through an angle twice the angle between the lines.
2×25∘=50∘2\times25^\circ = 50^\circ2×25∘=50∘.
Answer: A 50∘50^\circ50∘ rotation about the origin (the direction depends on the order of the two reflections).
Example 5: Why a composition of rigid motions is rigid
Explain why applying one rigid motion followed by another always produces another rigid motion.
Solution
Let T1T_1T1​ preserve distance: for any P,QP,QP,Q, the distance from T1(P)T_1(P)T1​(P) to T1(Q)T_1(Q)T1​(Q) equals the distance from PPP to QQQ.
Apply a second rigid motion T2T_2T2​ to those images: since T2T_2T2​ also preserves distance, the distance from T2(T1(P))T_2(T_1(P))T2​(T1​(P)) to T2(T1(Q))T_2(T_1(Q))T2​(T1​(Q)) equals the distance from T1(P)T_1(P)T1​(P) to T1(Q)T_1(Q)T1​(Q) — which already equals the distance from PPP to QQQ.
Chaining the two equalities shows the overall composition preserves distance too.
Answer: Distance is preserved at every stage, so the composition of any two rigid motions is itself a rigid motion.
Example 6: Rotate then reflect
Apply a 90∘90^\circ90∘ CCW rotation about the origin to (3,1)(3,1)(3,1), then reflect the result over the xxx-axis. Find the final image.
Solution
Apply each step in order, one at a time.
Rotate using (x,y)→(−y,x)(x,y)\to(-y,x)(x,y)→(−y,x): (3,1)→(−1,3)(3,1)\to(-1,3)(3,1)→(−1,3).
Reflect over the xxx-axis: (−1,3)→(−1,−3)(-1,3)\to(-1,-3)(−1,3)→(−1,−3).
Answer: (−1,−3)(-1,-3)(−1,−3).
Example 7: Confirming the intersecting-lines theorem with numbers
Reflect (4,1)(4,1)(4,1) over the xxx-axis, then reflect that image over the line y=xy=xy=x. Show the net result matches a single rotation about the origin, and state the angle.
Solution
Reflect over the xxx-axis: (4,1)→(4,−1)(4,1)\to(4,-1)(4,1)→(4,−1).
Reflect over y=xy=xy=x (swap coordinates): (4,−1)→(−1,4)(4,-1)\to(-1,4)(4,−1)→(−1,4).
Compare to the rotation rules: (4,1)→(−1,4)(4,1)\to(-1,4)(4,1)→(−1,4) matches the 90∘90^\circ90∘ CCW rule (x,y)→(−y,x)(x,y)\to(-y,x)(x,y)→(−y,x) exactly.
This confirms the theorem: the angle between the xxx-axis and y=xy=xy=x is 45∘45^\circ45∘, and 2×45∘=90∘2\times45^\circ=90^\circ2×45∘=90∘.
Answer: The composition equals a 90∘90^\circ90∘ CCW rotation about the origin.
Example 8: Reflecting twice over the same line
Reflect (3,7)(3,7)(3,7) over y=xy=xy=x, then reflect that image over y=xy=xy=x again. Explain why you always return to the start.
Solution
First reflection swaps coordinates: (3,7)→(7,3)(3,7)\to(7,3)(3,7)→(7,3).
Second reflection over the same line swaps them right back: (7,3)→(3,7)(7,3)\to(3,7)(7,3)→(3,7).
Answer: (3,7)(3,7)(3,7) — reflecting twice over the same line always undoes itself and produces the identity transformation.
Example 9: Testing for a true glide reflection
A transformation reflects over the xxx-axis, then translates by ⟨6,0⟩\langle6,0\rangle⟨6,0⟩. Is this a true glide reflection? Justify using the definition, then find the image of (2,3)(2,3)(2,3).
Solution
Definition check: a glide reflection requires the translation vector to be parallel to the line of reflection. Here the mirror is the xxx-axis (horizontal), and ⟨6,0⟩\langle6,0\rangle⟨6,0⟩ is also horizontal — the condition holds.
Apply to (2,3)(2,3)(2,3): reflect over the xxx-axis: (2,−3)(2,-3)(2,−3); translate by ⟨6,0⟩\langle6,0\rangle⟨6,0⟩: (8,−3)(8,-3)(8,−3).
Answer: Yes, this is a true glide reflection; the image of (2,3)(2,3)(2,3) is (8,−3)(8,-3)(8,−3).
Example 10: Reverse-engineer a composition
A composition of "reflect over the yyy-axis, then translate" maps (1,2)(1,2)(1,2) to (−3,5)(-3,5)(−3,5). Find the translation vector, then use it to find the image of (4,0)(4,0)(4,0) under the same composition.
Solution
Undo the reflection first to isolate the translation vector.
Undo the reflection to expose the translation: reflect (1,2)(1,2)(1,2) over the yyy-axis gives (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2).
The translation must carry (−1,2)(-1,2)(−1,2) to (−3,5)(-3,5)(−3,5): vector =⟨−3−(−1), 5−2⟩=⟨−2,3⟩=\langle-3-(-1),\ 5-2\rangle=\langle-2,3\rangle=⟨−3−(−1), 5−2⟩=⟨−2,3⟩.
Apply the full composition to (4,0)(4,0)(4,0): reflect over the yyy-axis: (−4,0)(-4,0)(−4,0); translate by ⟨−2,3⟩\langle-2,3\rangle⟨−2,3⟩: (−6,3)(-6,3)(−6,3).
Answer: translation vector ⟨−2,3⟩\langle-2,3\rangle⟨−2,3⟩; image of (4,0)(4,0)(4,0) is (−6,3)(-6,3)(−6,3).
