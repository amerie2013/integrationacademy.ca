12.5 Probability Models and Expected Value
A probability model assigns a probability to every outcome in a sample space; it's valid only if every probability is between 000 and 111 and they all sum to exactly 111. Theoretical probability comes from a formula or equally-likely-outcomes assumption; experimental probability comes from actual trial data (occurrencestrials\dfrac{\text{occurrences}}{\text{trials}}trialsoccurrences​) — the Law of Large Numbers says the experimental value tends to approach the theoretical one as trials increase. Expected value, E=∑(outcome×its probability)E=\sum(\text{outcome}\times\text{its probability})E=∑(outcome×its probability), is the long-run average result and is central to deciding whether a game, raffle, or insurance decision favors you or the house — even though it may never equal any single actual outcome.

Examples

Example 1: Checking a valid model
Outcomes AAA, BBB, CCC have probabilities 0.20.20.2, 0.50.50.5, 0.30.30.3. Is this a valid probability model?
Solution
Each bar is between 0 and 1, and together they account for the whole sample space.
Check two conditions for a valid model: (1) each probability is between 000 and 111 — yes for all three; (2) they sum to exactly 111: 0.2+0.5+0.3=10.2+0.5+0.3=10.2+0.5+0.3=1. ✓
Answer: Yes, valid.
Example 2: Spotting an invalid model
Probabilities 0.40.40.4, 0.40.40.4, 0.40.40.4 are proposed for three outcomes. Valid?
Solution
Each individual bar looks fine (between 0 and 1), but the required check is whether they sum to exactly 1.
Each value is between 000 and 111 on its own, but check the sum: 0.4+0.4+0.4=1.2≠10.4+0.4+0.4=1.2\neq10.4+0.4+0.4=1.2=1.
Answer: No — the probabilities must sum to exactly 111, and 1.2≠11.2\neq11.2=1.

Example 5: Expected value — two outcomes
A game pays $10 with probability 0.30.30.3, and $0 otherwise. Find the expected value.
Solution
Identify the formula: E=∑(outcome×its probability)E=\sum(\text{outcome}\times\text{its probability})E=∑(outcome×its probability), summed over every possible outcome.
Compute: E=10(0.3)+0(0.7)=3+0=3E=10(0.3)+0(0.7)=3+0=3E=10(0.3)+0(0.7)=3+0=3.
Answer: $3 — the long-run average payout per play.
Example 6: Is the game fair?
A game costs $4 to play and has expected payout $4. Is it fair?
Solution
A game is fair exactly when the expected payout equals the cost to play (expected net gain =0=0=0).
4=44=44=4, so yes.
Answer: Fair.
Example 7: Expected net gain (or loss)
A game costs $5 to play; it pays $20 with probability 0.20.20.2, and $0 otherwise. Find the expected net gain.
Solution
Step 1 — expected payout: E(payout)=20(0.2)+0(0.8)=4E(\text{payout})=20(0.2)+0(0.8)=4E(payout)=20(0.2)+0(0.8)=4.
Step 2 — expected net: subtract the cost: 4−5=−14-5=-14−5=−1.
Answer: expected net =−1=-1=−1 (an average loss of $1 per play — the game favors the house).
Example 8: Law of Large Numbers
As the number of trials increases, what happens to the experimental probability?
Solution
It tends to get closer and closer to the theoretical probability — large samples smooth out the random "noise" that small samples show (like Example 3's 0.450.450.45 vs. the true 0.50.50.5).
Answer: it approaches the theoretical probability.

Example 11: Expected value in a real decision — is a warranty worth it?
A $500 laptop has an 8%8\%8% chance of needing a $300 repair within a year. An extended warranty covering that repair costs $35. Using expected value, decide whether buying the warranty is the better choice on average.
Solution
Expected repair cost without the warranty: E=300(0.08)+0(0.92)=24E=300(0.08)+0(0.92)=24E=300(0.08)+0(0.92)=24.
Cost with the warranty: a guaranteed $35 (assuming it fully covers the repair).
Since the expected repair cost ($24) is less than the warranty ($35), skipping the warranty is cheaper on average — by 35−24=1135-24=1135−24=11 dollars.
Answer: skipping the warranty saves $11 in expectation. Caveat: expected value describes the long-run average — someone risk-averse might still prefer the warranty's certainty over the small chance of a $300 hit.
