11.3 Incircle and Circumcircle
Every triangle has exactly one incenter (where the three angle bisectors meet, always inside the triangle) and exactly one circumcenter (where the three perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet, which can be inside, on, or outside the triangle). The incenter is the center of the incircle — the largest circle that fits inside the triangle, tangent to all three sides — with radius r=Areasr=\dfrac{\text{Area}}{s}r=sArea​ (sss = semiperimeter). The circumcenter is the center of the circumcircle — the unique circle through all three vertices — with radius R=abc4⋅AreaR=\dfrac{abc}{4\cdot\text{Area}}R=4⋅Areaabc​. This worksheet constructs both centers and computes both radii, including with Heron's Formula for the area.

Examples

Example 1: Constructing the incenter — full justified steps
Construct the incenter of △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC.
Solution
The three angle bisectors of any triangle are concurrent — they always meet at one point, the incenter I.
  1. Construct the bisector of ∠A\angle A∠A (Lesson 11.2's method).
  2. Construct the bisector of ∠B\angle B∠B.
  3. The two bisectors intersect at a point — label it III. (The third bisector, from ∠C\angle C∠C, will automatically pass through III too — only two are needed to locate it.)
Justification: III lies on the bisector of ∠A\angle A∠A, so it is equidistant from sides AB‾\overline{AB}AB and AC‾\overline{AC}AC (Angle Bisector Theorem). III also lies on the bisector of ∠B\angle B∠B, so it is equidistant from AB‾\overline{AB}AB and BC‾\overline{BC}BC. Combining both, III is equidistant from all three sides — exactly what's needed to be the center of an inscribed circle.
Answer: III, the incenter, is equidistant from all three sides.
Example 2: Constructing the circumcenter — full justified steps
Construct the circumcenter of △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC.
Solution
The three perpendicular bisectors of the sides are also always concurrent — they meet at the circumcenter O.
  1. Construct the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB (Lesson 11.2's method).
  2. Construct the perpendicular bisector of BC‾\overline{BC}BC.
  3. The two bisectors intersect at a point — label it OOO. (The perpendicular bisector of AC‾\overline{AC}AC will also pass through OOO.)
Justification: OOO lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB, so OA=OBOA=OBOA=OB (Perpendicular Bisector Theorem). OOO also lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC‾\overline{BC}BC, so OB=OCOB=OCOB=OC. Combining both, OA=OB=OCOA=OB=OCOA=OB=OC — OOO is equidistant from all three vertices, so a circle centered at OOO through any one vertex passes through all three.
Answer: OOO, the circumcenter, is equidistant from all three vertices.
Example 3: Inradius via Heron's Formula
A triangle has sides 131313, 141414, 151515. Find its inradius.
Solution
Step 1 — semiperimeter: s=13+14+152=422=21s=\dfrac{13+14+15}{2}=\dfrac{42}{2}=21s=213+14+15​=242​=21.
Step 2 — area (Heron's Formula): Area=s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)=21(8)(7)(6)=7056=84\text{Area}=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}=\sqrt{21(8)(7)(6)}=\sqrt{7056}=84Area=s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)​=21(8)(7)(6)​=7056​=84.
Step 3 — inradius: r=Areas=8421=4r=\dfrac{\text{Area}}{s}=\dfrac{84}{21}=4r=sArea​=2184​=4.
Answer: r=4r=4r=4.

Example 5: Right triangle — circumcenter at the hypotenuse's midpoint
A right triangle has legs 666 and 888 (hypotenuse 101010). Find its circumradius.
Solution
Thales' Theorem (converse): the circumcenter of any right triangle is always the midpoint of the hypotenuse, so the circumradius is always half the hypotenuse.
R=102=5R=\dfrac{10}{2}=5R=210​=5.
Check with the formula: Area=12(6)(8)=24\text{Area}=\dfrac12(6)(8)=24Area=21​(6)(8)=24; R=6×8×104(24)=48096=5R=\dfrac{6\times8\times10}{4(24)}=\dfrac{480}{96}=5R=4(24)6×8×10​=96480​=5. ✓
Answer: R=5R=5R=5.
Example 6: Why the circumcenter moves inside/outside
Explain why the circumcenter is inside an acute triangle but outside an obtuse triangle.
Solution
Each perpendicular bisector is perpendicular to a side at its midpoint. In an acute triangle, all three angles are less than 90∘90^\circ90∘, which keeps all three perpendicular bisectors crossing within the triangle's interior. In an obtuse triangle, the obtuse angle "pushes" the triangle's shape so far that the perpendicular bisectors of the two sides forming that angle only meet outside the triangle, on the far side of the longest side. (In a right triangle, this boundary case lands exactly on the hypotenuse — Example 5.)
Answer: acute ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ inside; right ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ on the hypotenuse's midpoint; obtuse ⇒\Rightarrow⇒ outside.
Example 7: Equilateral triangle — incenter equals circumcenter
An equilateral triangle has side length 666. Find its inradius and circumradius, and describe how they compare.
Solution
For an equilateral triangle of side sss: r=s23r=\dfrac{s}{2\sqrt3}r=23​s​ and R=s3R=\dfrac{s}{\sqrt3}R=3​s​.
r=623=33=3r=\dfrac{6}{2\sqrt3}=\dfrac{3}{\sqrt3}=\sqrt3r=23​6​=3​3​=3​.
R=63=23R=\dfrac{6}{\sqrt3}=2\sqrt3R=3​6​=23​.
Notice R=2rR=2rR=2r exactly (23=2(3)2\sqrt3=2(\sqrt3)23​=2(3​)) — this R=2rR=2rR=2r relationship is special to equilateral triangles, and by symmetry the incenter and circumcenter are the very same point.
Answer: r=3r=\sqrt3r=3​; R=23R=2\sqrt3R=23​; both centers coincide.
Example 8: Finishing the incircle after locating $I$
After constructing the incenter III, describe the remaining steps to draw the actual incircle.
Solution
  1. From III, construct a perpendicular segment to any one side (e.g., drop a perpendicular from III to AB‾\overline{AB}AB, meeting it at point FFF).
  2. Set the compass to radius IFIFIF.
  3. Centered at III, draw the circle — it will be tangent to all three sides automatically, since III is equidistant from all three (Example 1).
Answer: measure the perpendicular distance from III to one side; that distance is the radius for the incircle.
Example 9: Word problem — the largest circular flower bed
A triangular garden has sides 999 m, 101010 m, and 171717 m. Find the radius of the largest circular flower bed that fits entirely inside it (tangent to all three fences).
Solution
This is the inradius. Step 1 — semiperimeter: s=9+10+172=18s=\dfrac{9+10+17}{2}=18s=29+10+17​=18.
Step 2 — area: Area=18(9)(8)(1)=1296=36\text{Area}=\sqrt{18(9)(8)(1)}=\sqrt{1296}=36Area=18(9)(8)(1)​=1296​=36.
Step 3 — inradius: r=3618=2r=\dfrac{36}{18}=2r=1836​=2.
Answer: the largest flower bed has radius 222 m.
Example 10: Word problem — equidistant cell tower
Three towns AAA, BBB, CCC (not in a line) need a single cell tower built equidistant from all three. Describe how to locate it, and what determines the tower's coverage radius.
Solution
The set of points equidistant from all three towns is exactly the circumcenter of △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC: construct the perpendicular bisectors of any two sides (say AB‾\overline{AB}AB and BC‾\overline{BC}BC); their intersection is the tower's location. The distance from that point to any one town (they're all equal) is the circumradius RRR, which is also the minimum broadcast radius needed to reach all three towns.
Answer: build at the circumcenter; the required coverage radius is the circumradius RRR.
