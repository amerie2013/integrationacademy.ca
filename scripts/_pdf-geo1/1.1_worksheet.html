1.1 Points, Lines, and Planes
Point, line, and plane are the three undefined terms that every later definition and postulate is built from. Beyond simply naming a ray or a segment, this section pushes into the logic behind collinearity and coplanarity — using postulates to justify why three points determine a plane only when they are noncollinear, and why two planes can only intersect in a line, never in a single stray point. Expect problems that combine several undefined terms in one figure, ask you to name the postulate that justifies an answer, and require reasoning about three-dimensional configurations rather than just flat sketches.

Examples

Example 1: Name a line two ways
Points A(1,4)A(1,4)A(1,4) and B(5,1)B(5,1)B(5,1) are graphed. Explain why exactly one line passes through both, then name that line in two ways.
Solution
Exactly one line can be drawn through A and B.
Identify the postulate: the Line Postulate states that through any two distinct points there is exactly one line — once AAA and BBB are fixed, the connecting line is uniquely determined.
A line extends without end in both directions and has no preferred starting point, so it may be named from either point.
Answer: AB↔\overleftrightarrow{AB}AB or BA↔\overleftrightarrow{BA}BA name the same line.
Example 2: Name a plane and check the condition
Points AAA, BBB, CCC do not lie on one line. Explain why they determine a plane, then name it.
Solution
Three noncollinear points pin down exactly one flat plane.
Check the condition: the Plane Postulate requires the three points to be noncollinear — that is given here.
Apply the postulate: three noncollinear points determine exactly one plane containing all of them.
Name the plane using any order of the three letters.
Answer: Plane ABCABCABC (equivalently ACBACBACB, BACBACBAC, etc.).
Example 3: Collinear vs. coplanar across two planes
In the figure, two planes are joined along AB‾\overline{AB}AB, with MMM between AAA and BBB, point CCC in the left plane, and point DDD in the right plane. (a) Are AAA, MMM, BBB collinear? (b) Are AAA, BBB, CCC, DDD all coplanar?
Solution
Two planes share the line through A, M, B; C and D sit in different planes.
(a) AAA, MMM, BBB all lie on the shared edge, which is a single line, so they are collinear.
(b) CCC belongs only to the left plane and DDD belongs only to the right plane; one flat plane cannot contain points from both slanted surfaces at once, so A,B,C,DA,B,C,DA,B,C,D are not coplanar.
Lesson: coplanar must hold for the whole set at once — it is not enough that some triples within it are coplanar.
Example 4: Opposite rays
AAA, MMM, BBB are collinear with MMM between AAA and BBB. Are MA→\overrightarrow{MA}MA and MB→\overrightarrow{MB}MB opposite rays? What about AM→\overrightarrow{AM}AM and MB→\overrightarrow{MB}MB?
Solution
M lies between A and B on one line.
Check the definition: opposite rays must share the same endpoint and point in exactly opposite directions along one line.
MA→\overrightarrow{MA}MA and MB→\overrightarrow{MB}MB both start at MMM and head toward opposite ends of the line — both conditions hold, so they are opposite rays.
AM→\overrightarrow{AM}AM starts at AAA, not MMM, so it does not share an endpoint with MB→\overrightarrow{MB}MB — not opposite rays, even though all three points lie on the same line.
Answer: MA→,MB→\overrightarrow{MA},\overrightarrow{MB}MA,MB are opposite; AM→,MB→\overrightarrow{AM},\overrightarrow{MB}AM,MB are not.
Example 5: Uniqueness of a line
Explain, using a postulate, why two distinct points cannot lie on two different lines.
Solution
State the postulate: the Line Postulate guarantees exactly one line through any two distinct points — the word "exactly" rules out more than one.
Suppose, for contradiction, that two different lines ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ and ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​ both passed through points AAA and BBB. That would contradict the postulate, which promises only one such line.
Conclusion: two distinct points determine one line, and only one — never two.
Example 6: A point off a line determines a plane
Line ℓ\ellℓ contains points DDD, EEE, FFF. Point GGG is not on ℓ\ellℓ. How many planes contain both ℓ\ellℓ and GGG? Justify your answer.
Solution
Reduce to the three-point case: GGG together with any two points of ℓ\ellℓ (say DDD and EEE) forms a noncollinear triple, since GGG is not on ℓ\ellℓ.
Apply the postulate: three noncollinear points determine exactly one plane, and that same plane contains all of ℓ\ellℓ (since D,E∈ℓD,E \in \ellD,E∈ℓ forces the whole line in, by the Line-in-Plane Postulate).
Answer: Exactly one plane contains both ℓ\ellℓ and GGG.
Example 7: Two intersecting lines determine a plane
Lines jjj and kkk intersect at point PPP. Explain why jjj and kkk must lie in a common plane, and how many such planes exist.
Solution
Pick a second point on each line: Q≠PQ \neq PQ=P on jjj, and R≠PR \neq PR=P on kkk.
PPP, QQQ, RRR are noncollinear (since QQQ lies only on jjj and RRR lies only on kkk, and j≠kj\neq kj=k), so they determine exactly one plane.
That plane contains PPP and QQQ, so by the Line-in-Plane Postulate it contains all of line jjj; it contains PPP and RRR, so it contains all of line kkk.
Conclusion: intersecting lines always lie in exactly one common plane.
Example 8: Counting planes from four points
Four points AAA, BBB, CCC, DDD are positioned so that no three are collinear, and the four points are not all coplanar. How many different planes are determined by choosing 3 of the 4 points at a time?
Solution
Count the ways to choose 3 points from 4: (43)=4\binom{4}{3} = 4(34​)=4 triples — ABCABCABC, ABDABDABD, ACDACDACD, BCDBCDBCD.
Since no three points are collinear, each triple is noncollinear and determines exactly one plane.
Because the four points are not all coplanar, no single plane can contain more than 3 of them, so the four triples give four different planes — like the four triangular faces of a tetrahedron.
Answer: 444 planes.
Example 9: Real-world model — a room corner
In a rectangular room, the floor and two adjacent walls meet at a corner. Model the corner, the wall-to-wall edge, and each flat surface using undefined terms, then describe (a) the intersection of the two walls and (b) the intersection of all three surfaces.
Solution
Model: each flat surface (floor, each wall) is a plane; the edge where the two walls meet is a line; the corner where all three surfaces meet is a point.
(a) Two planes (the two walls) intersect in a line — the vertical edge where the walls meet.
(b) All three planes share only the single corner point, since the vertical wall edge touches the floor at just that one spot.
Takeaway: two planes meet in a line, but three planes in general position meet in a single point.
Example 10: Line–plane intersection
Line ttt is not contained in plane P\mathcal{P}P, but ttt does cross through P\mathcal{P}P. What is t∩Pt \cap \mathcal{P}t∩P, and why can it not be more than that?
Solution
Since ttt is not contained in P\mathcal{P}P, at least one point of ttt lies off the plane. If ttt shared two or more points with P\mathcal{P}P, the Line-in-Plane Postulate would force all of ttt into P\mathcal{P}P — a contradiction.
So ttt shares at most one point with P\mathcal{P}P; since we are told it does cross the plane, it shares exactly one.
Answer: t∩Pt \cap \mathcal{P}t∩P is a single point.
