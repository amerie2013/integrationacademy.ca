4.3 SSS and SAS Congruence
Two triangles are congruent when there is a correspondence between their vertices such that all corresponding sides and angles match. Rather than checking all six parts, shortcut postulates let us prove congruence from partial information. The SSS Postulate says three pairs of congruent corresponding sides are enough. The SAS Postulate says two pairs of congruent sides plus the included angle (the angle directly between those two sides) are enough. The angle's position matters: if the given angle is not between the two given sides (the "SSA" case), the triangle is not guaranteed to be unique — this is why SSA is not a valid congruence shortcut. Shared sides use the Reflexive Property, and intersecting segments often supply a hidden congruent angle pair via the Vertical Angles Theorem. This worksheet applies SSS and SAS with numeric, algebraic, and coordinate reasoning.

Examples

Example 1: SSS from a diagonal
Quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has diagonal AC‾\overline{AC}AC. Given AB=CD=6AB=CD=6AB=CD=6 and BC=DA=9BC=DA=9BC=DA=9, prove △ABC≅△CDA\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA△ABC≅△CDA.
Solution
One triangle formed by the diagonal; tick marks show a pair of congruent sides.
List the three pairs of corresponding sides:
AB=CD=6AB=CD=6AB=CD=6 (given)
BC=DA=9BC=DA=9BC=DA=9 (given)
AC=CAAC=CAAC=CA (Reflexive Property — shared diagonal).
All three pairs of corresponding sides are congruent.
Conclusion: △ABC≅△CDA\triangle ABC \cong \triangle CDA△ABC≅△CDA by SSS.
Example 2: Why the included angle matters
A triangle has sides 555 and 888 with the angle between them measuring 40∘40^{\circ}40∘. Explain why this fixes a unique triangle, while knowing sides 555 and 888 with a 40∘40^{\circ}40∘ angle opposite the side of length 555 (not between the two sides) would not.
Solution
When the 40∘40^{\circ}40∘ angle is included (between the two given sides), the two sides are locked at that fixed angle, so their endpoints can only be joined by one possible third side — the triangle is unique. This is the SAS Postulate.
When the given angle is not included (adjacent to only one of the two sides), the second side can often "swing" and land in two different positions while still satisfying all the given measurements, producing two non-congruent triangles.
Conclusion: this ambiguity is exactly why SSA is not accepted as a congruence postulate, while SAS is.

Example 5: Vertical angles supply the included angle
Segments AC‾\overline{AC}AC and BD‾\overline{BD}BD intersect at EEE. Given AE=CEAE=CEAE=CE and BE=DEBE=DEBE=DE, prove △AEB≅△CED\triangle AEB \cong \triangle CED△AEB≅△CED.
Solution
Identify the given congruent sides: AE=CEAE=CEAE=CE and BE=DEBE=DEBE=DE.
∠AEB\angle AEB∠AEB and ∠CED\angle CED∠CED are vertical angles (formed by the two intersecting segments), so ∠AEB≅∠CED\angle AEB \cong \angle CED∠AEB≅∠CED by the Vertical Angles Theorem.
This angle is included between AE‾,BE‾\overline{AE}, \overline{BE}AE,BE in one triangle and CE‾,DE‾\overline{CE}, \overline{DE}CE,DE in the other.
Two pairs of sides and the included angle are congruent.
Conclusion: △AEB≅△CED\triangle AEB \cong \triangle CED△AEB≅△CED by SAS.
Example 6: Parallelogram diagonal SSS
Parallelogram ABCDABCDABCD has diagonal BD‾\overline{BD}BD. Opposite sides of a parallelogram are congruent, so AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD and AD=CBAD=CBAD=CB. Prove △ABD≅△CDB\triangle ABD \cong \triangle CDB△ABD≅△CDB.
Solution
List the three pairs of corresponding sides:
AB=CDAB=CDAB=CD (opposite sides of parallelogram)
AD=CBAD=CBAD=CB (opposite sides of parallelogram)
BD=DBBD=DBBD=DB (Reflexive Property — shared diagonal).
All three pairs of sides are congruent.
Conclusion: △ABD≅△CDB\triangle ABD \cong \triangle CDB△ABD≅△CDB by SSS.
Example 7: CPCTC after SSS
△ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF by SSS, with ∠A=50∘\angle A=50^{\circ}∠A=50∘ and ∠B=65∘\angle B=65^{\circ}∠B=65∘. Find ∠D\angle D∠D, ∠E\angle E∠E, and ∠F\angle F∠F.
Solution
The congruence statement △ABC≅△DEF\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF△ABC≅△DEF tells the correspondence: A↔DA\leftrightarrow DA↔D, B↔EB\leftrightarrow EB↔E, C↔FC\leftrightarrow FC↔F.
By CPCTC (Corresponding Parts of Congruent Triangles are Congruent): ∠D=∠A=50∘\angle D=\angle A=50^{\circ}∠D=∠A=50∘ and ∠E=∠B=65∘\angle E=\angle B=65^{\circ}∠E=∠B=65∘.
Find ∠C\angle C∠C using the Angle-Sum Theorem: ∠C=180−50−65=65∘\angle C=180-50-65=65^{\circ}∠C=180−50−65=65∘.
By CPCTC again, ∠F=∠C=65∘\angle F=\angle C=65^{\circ}∠F=∠C=65∘.
Answer: ∠D=50∘\angle D=50^{\circ}∠D=50∘, ∠E=65∘\angle E=65^{\circ}∠E=65∘, ∠F=65∘\angle F=65^{\circ}∠F=65∘.
Example 8: When no postulate applies (SSA)
In △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC and △DEF\triangle DEF△DEF: AB=DEAB=DEAB=DE, BC=EFBC=EFBC=EF, and ∠A≅∠D\angle A \cong \angle D∠A≅∠D, where ∠A\angle A∠A is not included between the two given sides. Can you conclude the triangles are congruent?
Solution
Check which parts are given: two pairs of sides (AB=DEAB=DEAB=DE, BC=EFBC=EFBC=EF) and one pair of angles (∠A≅∠D\angle A \cong \angle D∠A≅∠D).
The angle is adjacent to only one of the given sides, not included between both — this is the SSA arrangement.
As shown in Example 2, SSA can produce two different triangles from the same measurements.
Conclusion: No — congruence cannot be concluded from SSA (this is not a valid postulate, apart from the special right-triangle HL case).
Example 9: Truss manufacturing (SSS in context)
A truss manufacturer builds two triangular braces to the same specifications: side lengths 777 ft, 777 ft, and 101010 ft. Explain why the two braces must be congruent, and name the postulate.
Solution
Both braces have matching corresponding side lengths: 7=77=77=7, 7=77=77=7, and 10=1010=1010=10.
Three pairs of corresponding sides are congruent, regardless of how each brace is oriented on the factory floor.
Conclusion: the braces are congruent by SSS — matching all three side lengths always produces the same triangle shape.
