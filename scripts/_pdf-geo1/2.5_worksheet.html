2.5 Constructing Parallel & Perpendicular
Compass-and-straightedge constructions produce results that are exactly correct, provable through triangle congruence, rather than approximately correct from a measured reading. This section pushes past memorizing steps by asking why each construction works — usually because two equal-radius arcs force an SSS congruence, which in turn forces a right angle or equal segment. It also chains the corresponding-angle idea from Section 2.2 into an actual construction for a parallel line, and applies the perpendicular bisector to a genuine problem: locating the center of a circle from three points on it.

Examples

Example 1: Tools of a construction
What two tools are allowed in a classical geometric construction, and why are a ruler's numbered markings and a protractor's degree markings both excluded?
Solution
The two allowed tools are a compass (draws circles and arcs of a fixed radius) and an unmarked straightedge (draws straight lines, but does not measure).
Numbered rulers and protractors are excluded because a construction must be logically exact — reading "5.0 cm" or "37^\circ" off a scale is only as good as the tool's smallest marking, an approximation.
Answer: Compass and straightedge only, because they reproduce exact equal lengths and equal angles without relying on any measurement.
Example 2: Perpendicular at a point on a line
Describe the steps to construct a perpendicular to line ℓ\ellℓ at a point PPP on ℓ\ellℓ, and explain why the result is guaranteed to be exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Solution
Equal arcs from two equidistant points meet above and below, pinning down a perpendicular.
Steps: (1) center the compass at PPP and draw arcs of equal radius crossing ℓ\ellℓ on both sides, at AAA and BBB; (2) from AAA and from BBB, draw two more equal-radius arcs (wider than half ABABAB) that intersect above PPP, at QQQ; (3) draw PQ→\overrightarrow{PQ}PQ​.
Reasoning: PA=PBPA = PBPA=PB (first radius) and QA=QBQA = QBQA=QB (second radius), with PQ=PQPQ = PQPQ=PQ shared, so △QPA≅△QPB\triangle QPA \cong \triangle QPB△QPA≅△QPB by SSS — their angles at PPP are congruent and form a linear pair, forcing each to be 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Answer: PQ→⊥ℓ\overrightarrow{PQ} \perp \ellPQ​⊥ℓ, guaranteed exact by SSS congruence, not by measuring.
Example 3: Perpendicular bisector locates the midpoint too
Construct the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB, and explain why the point where it crosses AB‾\overline{AB}AB is exactly the midpoint.
Solution
The same construction proves perpendicularity and the midpoint at once.
Steps: from AAA and separately from BBB, draw arcs of equal radius (more than half ABABAB) intersecting above and below the segment at PPP and QQQ; draw PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​.
Since AP=BPAP = BPAP=BP and AQ=BQAQ = BQAQ=BQ (equal compass radii), with PQ=PQPQ=PQPQ=PQ shared, △APQ≅△BPQ\triangle APQ \cong \triangle BPQ△APQ≅△BPQ by SSS, forcing ∠APQ≅∠BPQ\angle APQ \cong \angle BPQ∠APQ≅∠BPQ — a congruent linear pair, so each is 90∘90^\circ90∘; CPCTC then gives AM=BMAM = BMAM=BM at the crossing point MMM.
Answer: PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​ is perpendicular to AB‾\overline{AB}AB at its midpoint MMM — one construction proves both facts.
Example 4: Perpendicular from a point not on the line
Describe the steps to drop a perpendicular from a point PPP (not on line ℓ\ellℓ) to ℓ\ellℓ.
Solution
Steps: (1) from PPP, draw an arc that crosses ℓ\ellℓ at two points, AAA and BBB; (2) from AAA and from BBB, draw two more equal-radius arcs on the opposite side of ℓ\ellℓ from PPP that intersect at QQQ; (3) draw PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​.
Reasoning: PA=PBPA = PBPA=PB from step 1 and QA=QBQA = QBQA=QB from step 2, so both PPP and QQQ are equidistant from AAA and BBB — meaning PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​ is the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB, which is therefore perpendicular to ℓ\ellℓ (since A,B∈ℓA, B \in \ellA,B∈ℓ).
Answer: PQ↔\overleftrightarrow{PQ}PQ​ meets ℓ\ellℓ at a right angle — the foot of the perpendicular from PPP.
Example 5: Copying an angle
Describe how to copy ∠A\angle A∠A onto a new ray using only compass and straightedge, and explain why the copy is exactly congruent.
Solution
Matching all three sides of the small triangle forces matching angles.
Steps: (1) draw an arc centered at the vertex of ∠A\angle A∠A, crossing both rays at BBB and CCC; (2) draw a new ray, and with the same compass radius, draw an arc from the new vertex crossing the new ray at B′B'B′; (3) open the compass to width BCBCBC, and from B′B'B′ draw an arc crossing the first new arc at C′C'C′; (4) draw the ray through C′C'C′.
Reasoning: the new triangle (vertex, B′B'B′, C′C'C′) has all three sides equal to the original (vertex, BBB, CCC) triangle by construction, so they are congruent by SSS, forcing the angles at the vertices to match.
Answer: The new angle is exactly congruent to ∠A\angle A∠A, guaranteed by SSS — not by eyeballing it.
Example 6: Constructing a parallel line
Explain how copying a corresponding angle at a new point PPP lets you construct a line through PPP parallel to a given line ℓ\ellℓ.
Solution
Draw a transversal through PPP that also crosses ℓ\ellℓ, creating an angle at ℓ\ellℓ. Using the Example 5 method, copy that angle at PPP, in the matching corresponding position along the same transversal.
The new angle is congruent to the original by construction, so the two lines now have congruent corresponding angles with the same transversal.
By the Converse of the Corresponding Angles Postulate (Section 2.2), congruent corresponding angles force the lines to be parallel.
Answer: The newly drawn line through PPP is parallel to ℓ\ellℓ.
Example 7: Why constructions outrank measuring
A ruler reads to the nearest millimeter and a protractor to the nearest degree. Explain why a compass-and-straightedge construction counts as more 'exact,' even though a compass is a physical tool too.
Solution
A ruler or protractor reading is inherently an approximation — the true value could fall anywhere within the tool's smallest marked interval.
A compass construction instead relies on logical guarantees, like SSS congruence, that hold exactly regardless of how carefully the compass was drawn — the proof guarantees the result, not the drawing's precision.
Answer: Constructions are exact because their correctness is proved through congruence, not read off a scale.
Example 8: Uniqueness of the perpendicular
Through a given point, how many lines can be drawn perpendicular to a given line? Justify your answer using the constructions above.
Solution
Both perpendicular constructions above (Examples 2 and 4) always produce one specific line — nothing in the steps allows a second, different perpendicular through the same point.
This matches the Uniqueness of Perpendiculars Theorem.
Answer: Exactly one perpendicular line through a given point.
Example 9: Uniqueness of the parallel
Through a point PPP not on line ℓ\ellℓ, how many lines can be drawn parallel to ℓ\ellℓ in Euclidean geometry?
Solution
The construction in Example 6 produces exactly one specific parallel line through PPP.
Euclidean geometry's Parallel Postulate (Playfair's form) guarantees that no second, different parallel line through PPP exists.
Answer: Exactly one — this uniqueness is special to Euclidean geometry.
Example 10: Application — finding a circle's center
Three points AAA, BBB, CCC lie on a circle whose center is unmarked. Explain how two perpendicular-bisector constructions can locate the exact center.
Solution
The center is equidistant from A and B — so it lies on this exact line.
The center of a circle is equidistant from every point on it, so it must be equidistant from AAA and BBB — meaning it lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB‾\overline{AB}AB.
By the same reasoning, it must also lie on the perpendicular bisector of BC‾\overline{BC}BC.
Construct both bisectors (Example 3's method, applied twice); their single intersection point is equidistant from all three points, so it must be the center.
Answer: The center is the intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of AB‾\overline{AB}AB and BC‾\overline{BC}BC.
