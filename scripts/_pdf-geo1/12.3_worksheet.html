12.3 Conditional Probability
Conditional probability asks: given that event BBB has already happened, how likely is AAA? The formula is P(A∣B)=P(A and B)P(B)P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A and B)​ — restrict attention to the world where BBB is true, then ask what fraction of that world also has AAA. Two-way (contingency) tables make this concrete: read down a column (or across a row) to get the reduced sample space. A key fact worth remembering: P(A∣B)P(A\mid B)P(A∣B) and P(B∣A)P(B\mid A)P(B∣A) are generally not equal — order matters. When P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A), knowing BBB told you nothing new about AAA, and the events are independent (Lesson 12.2).

Examples

Example 1: Formula, straight from given probabilities
P(A and B)=0.2P(A\text{ and }B)=0.2P(A and B)=0.2 and P(B)=0.5P(B)=0.5P(B)=0.5. Find P(A∣B)P(A\mid B)P(A∣B).
Solution
Identify the formula: P(A∣B)=P(A and B)P(B)P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\text{ and }B)}{P(B)}P(A∣B)=P(B)P(A and B)​ — restrict attention to the world where BBB happens, then see what fraction of it also has AAA.
Substitute and compute: P(A∣B)=0.20.5=0.4P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{0.2}{0.5}=0.4P(A∣B)=0.50.2​=0.4.
Answer: 0.40.40.4.

Example 3: Cards
A card is drawn from a standard deck. Find P(king∣face card)P(\text{king}\mid\text{face card})P(king∣face card).
Solution
Face cards are J, Q, K in all 444 suits: 121212 face cards total. Of those, 444 are kings.
P(king∣face card)=412=13P(\text{king}\mid\text{face card})=\dfrac{4}{12}=\dfrac13P(king∣face card)=124​=31​.

Example 4: Checking independence
P(A)=0.3P(A)=0.3P(A)=0.3 and P(A∣B)=0.3P(A\mid B)=0.3P(A∣B)=0.3. Are AAA and BBB independent?
Solution
Independence means knowing BBB doesn't change AAA's probability: P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(A∣B)=P(A).
Here 0.3=0.30.3=0.30.3=0.3, so yes — independent.
Answer: Independent.
Example 5: Dice
A fair die is rolled. Find P(even∣greater than 3)P(\text{even}\mid\text{greater than }3)P(even∣greater than 3).
Solution
Blue outline = the conditioning set ("greater than 3"); orange fill = also even.
"Given greater than 333" restricts the sample space to {4,5,6}\{4,5,6\}{4,5,6} — only 333 outcomes now. Of those, {4,6}\{4,6\}{4,6} are even.
P(even∣>3)=23P(\text{even}\mid>3)=\dfrac{2}{3}P(even∣>3)=32​.
Answer: 23\dfrac2332​.
Example 6: A conditioning event that forces certainty
A bag has 333 red and 777 blue marbles. One is drawn. Find P(red∣not blue)P(\text{red}\mid\text{not blue})P(red∣not blue).
Solution
"Not blue" in this bag means the marble must be red — there's no third color. So given "not blue," red happens every single time.
P(red∣not blue)=1P(\text{red}\mid\text{not blue})=1P(red∣not blue)=1.
Answer: 111.
Example 7: Order matters — the reverse conditional
Using the same table from Example 2, find P(sports∣girl)P(\text{sports}\mid\text{girl})P(sports∣girl) and compare it to P(girl∣sports)=58P(\text{girl}\mid\text{sports})=\dfrac58P(girl∣sports)=85​ from Example 2.
Solution
Now condition on "girl" — restrict to the Girls row: 606060 girls total, of whom 252525 play sports.
P(sports∣girl)=2560=512P(\text{sports}\mid\text{girl})=\dfrac{25}{60}=\dfrac{5}{12}P(sports∣girl)=6025​=125​.
Compare: P(girl∣sports)=58=0.625P(\text{girl}\mid\text{sports})=\dfrac58=0.625P(girl∣sports)=85​=0.625, but P(sports∣girl)=512≈0.417P(\text{sports}\mid\text{girl})=\dfrac{5}{12}\approx0.417P(sports∣girl)=125​≈0.417. These are different — conditioning on the column vs. the row gives different restricted sample spaces.
Answer: 512\dfrac{5}{12}125​; it is not equal to P(girl∣sports)P(\text{girl}\mid\text{sports})P(girl∣sports).

Example 10: Using conditional probability to test independence
In a survey of 606060 teens, 363636 have phones, and 242424 of the phone owners are girls. Overall, 303030 of the 606060 teens are girls. Determine whether "having a phone" and "being a girl" are independent in this survey.
Solution
Overall rate: P(girl)=3060=12P(\text{girl})=\dfrac{30}{60}=\dfrac12P(girl)=6030​=21​.
Conditional rate: P(girl∣phone)=2436=23P(\text{girl}\mid\text{phone})=\dfrac{24}{36}=\dfrac23P(girl∣phone)=3624​=32​.
Since 23≠12\dfrac23\neq\dfrac1232​=21​, knowing someone has a phone does change the probability they're a girl — in this survey, the two categories are dependent, not independent.
Answer: Dependent, since P(girl∣phone)=23≠P(girl)=12P(\text{girl}\mid\text{phone})=\dfrac23\neq P(\text{girl})=\dfrac12P(girl∣phone)=32​=P(girl)=21​.
Example 11: A "90% accurate" test isn't what it sounds like
A disease affects 1%1\%1% of a population. A test for it is 90%90\%90% accurate both ways (it correctly identifies 90%90\%90% of people who have the disease, and correctly clears 90%90\%90% of people who don't). Given a positive test result, find P(disease∣positive)P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})P(disease∣positive).
Solution
Imagine a population of 10,00010{,}00010,000 for concreteness.
Have the disease: 1%1\%1% of 10,000=10010{,}000=10010,000=100 people. Of these, 90%90\%90% test positive: 909090 true positives (and 101010 false negatives).
Don't have it: 990099009900 people. Of these, 10%10\%10% test positive anyway (false positive): 0.10×9900=9900.10\times9900=9900.10×9900=990 false positives.
Total positive tests: 90+990=108090+990=108090+990=1080.
P(disease∣positive)=901080=112P(\text{disease}\mid\text{positive})=\dfrac{90}{1080}=\dfrac{1}{12}P(disease∣positive)=108090​=121​.
Answer: 112\dfrac{1}{12}121​ — only about 8%8\%8%! Even an "accurate" test gives mostly false alarms when the disease is rare, because the huge healthy population produces far more false positives than the small sick population produces true positives.
