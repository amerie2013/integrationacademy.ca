11.1 Copying Segments and Angles
Classical constructions use only two tools: an unmarked straightedge (draws lines, but never measures) and a compass (transfers a fixed distance). Every construction is a sequence of steps that can each be justified — usually by showing that the construction forces two triangles to be congruent (often by SSS). This worksheet builds the two most fundamental constructions — copying a segment and copying an angle — and uses them to copy an entire triangle. Each example gives a full, justified step list, not just a final picture: on a real exam you must be able to explain why each step guarantees congruence, not just perform it.

Examples

Example 1: Copy a segment — full justified steps
Copy AB‾\overline{AB}AB onto a ray with endpoint A′A'A′.
Solution
The compass carries the exact length ABABAB to the new ray without ever needing to measure a number.
  1. Draw a ray starting at A′A'A′. (This ray will contain the copy.)
  2. Open the compass to width ABABAB: point on AAA, pencil on BBB. (The compass now physically stores the length ABABAB.)
  3. Without changing the compass width, place the point on A′A'A′ and swing an arc crossing the ray.
  4. Label the crossing point B′B'B′.
Justification: the compass radius never changed between steps 2 and 3, so the distance from A′A'A′ to every point on the arc — including B′B'B′ — equals ABABAB. Therefore A′B′‾≅AB‾\overline{A'B'}\cong\overline{AB}A′B′≅AB.
Answer: A′B′‾≅AB‾\overline{A'B'}\cong\overline{AB}A′B′≅AB by construction.
Example 2: Why the straightedge stays unmarked
A student copying PQ‾=7\overline{PQ}=7PQ​=7 cm says, "I'll just use a ruler and mark 777 cm on the new ray." Explain why this is not a valid classical construction, even though the length is correct.
Solution
A classical construction must work using only compass and straightedge — no numbers, no marked lengths. Using a ruler's tick marks to count out "777 cm" uses measurement, which was historically considered a different (less rigorous) tool than the compass.
The compass method proves congruence directly from the unchanging radius (see Example 1), with no dependence on a numbered scale — it would work identically if the segment were relabeled in different units.
Answer: Not valid — it uses measurement, not compass-transferred congruence, even though the resulting length happens to be correct.
Example 3: Copy an angle — full justified steps
Copy ∠DBE\angle DBE∠DBE to a new vertex B′B'B′.
Solution
Copying the chord length D′E′D'E'D′E′ forces the new angle to match the original by SSS.
  1. Draw a ray from B′B'B′ (this becomes one side of the new angle).
  2. At the original vertex BBB, draw an arc of any radius crossing both rays of ∠DBE\angle DBE∠DBE, at points DDD and EEE.
  3. Without changing that radius, draw the same arc centered at B′B'B′, crossing the new ray at D′D'D′.
  4. Open the compass to width DEDEDE (the chord between the two original arc points).
  5. Centered at D′D'D′, swing an arc of radius DEDEDE crossing the new arc; label the intersection E′E'E′.
  6. Draw ray B′E′→\overrightarrow{B'E'}B′E′.
Justification: BD=B′D′BD=B'D'BD=B′D′ and BE=B′E′BE=B'E'BE=B′E′ (same compass radius as steps 2–3), and DE=D′E′DE=D'E'DE=D′E′ (step 4–5). So △BDE≅△B′D′E′\triangle BDE\cong\triangle B'D'E'△BDE≅△B′D′E′ by SSS, which forces ∠DBE≅∠D′B′E′\angle DBE\cong\angle D'B'E'∠DBE≅∠D′B′E′ (corresponding parts of congruent triangles).
Answer: ∠D′B′E′≅∠DBE\angle D'B'E'\cong\angle DBE∠D′B′E′≅∠DBE.
Example 4: Algebra check on a copied angle
An angle of measure (3x+10)∘(3x+10)^\circ(3x+10)∘ is copied exactly. If the copy measures 70∘70^\circ70∘, find xxx.
Solution
Since the copy is congruent to the original, the two measures are equal:
3x+10=703x+10=703x+10=70
3x=603x=603x=60
x=20x=20x=20.
Answer: x=20x=20x=20.
Example 5: Why the compass width can't drift mid-construction
During the segment-copy construction, a student's compass slips slightly wider between steps 2 and 3. Explain what goes wrong.
Solution
The entire justification in Example 1 depends on one fact: the radius used to swing the arc from A′A'A′ equals the radius that was set from AAA to BBB. If the compass slips wider, the arc from A′A'A′ now represents some length greater than ABABAB, so the resulting B′B'B′ would satisfy A′B′>ABA'B'>ABA′B′>AB — the segments would no longer be congruent, defeating the purpose of the construction.
Answer: The copied segment would be too long (or too short if it slipped narrower); it would fail to be congruent to the original.
Example 6: SSS triangle copy — full justified steps
Given △ABC\triangle ABC△ABC, construct △DEF≅△ABC\triangle DEF\cong\triangle ABC△DEF≅△ABC using only compass and straightedge.
Solution
Copying all three side lengths pins down the triangle completely (SSS).
  1. Draw a ray and copy AB‾\overline{AB}AB onto it (Example 1's method), labeling the endpoints DDD and EEE, so DE=ABDE=ABDE=AB.
  2. Open the compass to width ACACAC. Centered at DDD, draw an arc.
  3. Open the compass to width BCBCBC. Centered at EEE, draw a second arc, intersecting the first.
  4. Label the intersection point FFF, and draw DF‾\overline{DF}DF and EF‾\overline{EF}EF.
Justification: by construction, DE=ABDE=ABDE=AB, DF=ACDF=ACDF=AC, and EF=BCEF=BCEF=BC — all three pairs of sides are congruent, so △DEF≅△ABC\triangle DEF\cong\triangle ABC△DEF≅△ABC by SSS.
Answer: △DEF≅△ABC\triangle DEF\cong\triangle ABC△DEF≅△ABC by SSS.
Example 7: Checking feasibility before constructing
Before attempting an SSS triangle copy with side lengths 444, 555, and 101010, check whether a triangle with these side lengths can even exist.
Solution
By the Triangle Inequality Theorem, the sum of any two sides must exceed the third side. Test the two shortest sides against the longest:
4+5=94+5=94+5=9, but the third side is 101010, and 9<109<109<10.
Since the inequality fails, no triangle can be formed — in the construction, the two arcs (radius 444 from one endpoint, radius 555 from the other, on a base of 101010) would never intersect.
Answer: No triangle exists; the construction's arcs would fail to meet.
Example 8: Why the angle-copy method actually works
Explain in one sentence why swinging equal arcs at both vertices and matching the chord distance guarantees the two angles are congruent — without just saying "SSS."
Solution
The two equal-radius arcs make BD=B′D′BD=B'D'BD=B′D′ and BE=B′E′BE=B'E'BE=B′E′, and copying the chord makes DE=D′E′DE=D'E'DE=D′E′; three pairs of equal sides between △BDE\triangle BDE△BDE and △B′D′E′\triangle B'D'E'△B′D′E′ force the triangles to be congruent (SSS), and since ∠DBE\angle DBE∠DBE and ∠D′B′E′\angle D'B'E'∠D′B′E′ are the angles included between the equal sides, they must be the corresponding congruent angles of those triangles.
Answer: the two arc points and the chord create three congruent side pairs (SSS), and the angle in question is the included angle between the equal-radius sides in each triangle.
Example 9: Copying an obtuse angle
∠XYZ\angle XYZ∠XYZ measures 130∘130^\circ130∘ (obtuse). Does the copying method from Example 3 need to change for an obtuse angle?
Solution
No. The construction never refers to whether the angle is acute, right, or obtuse — it only uses arc radii and a chord length, which work identically regardless of angle measure. The only visual difference is that the two rays open wider apart.
Answer: No changes needed; the same steps copy any angle from 0∘0^\circ0∘ to 180∘180^\circ180∘.
Example 10: Building a segment as a sum of two copies
Segments PQ‾\overline{PQ}PQ​ (length 555) and RS‾\overline{RS}RS (length 888) are given. Construct a single segment of length 131313 using only compass and straightedge (no ruler).
Solution
  1. Draw a ray from a point XXX.
  2. Copy PQ‾\overline{PQ}PQ​ onto the ray starting at XXX: swing an arc of radius PQPQPQ from XXX, landing at YYY, so XY=5XY=5XY=5.
  3. Copy RS‾\overline{RS}RS onto the same ray, starting at YYY (not XXX): swing an arc of radius RSRSRS from YYY, landing at ZZZ, so YZ=8YZ=8YZ=8.
By the Segment Addition Postulate, XZ=XY+YZ=5+8=13XZ=XY+YZ=5+8=13XZ=XY+YZ=5+8=13.
Answer: XZ‾\overline{XZ}XZ has length 131313, built entirely by two compass copies laid end to end — no ruler needed.
