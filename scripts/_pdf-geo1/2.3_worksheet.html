2.3 Theorems about Perpendicular Lines
Perpendicularity is a stronger statement than just "one right angle" — it forces all four angles at the intersection to be 90∘90^\circ90∘, which is what lets a single right-angle mark unlock an entire figure. This section links perpendicularity to parallelism in two directions: two lines each perpendicular to a third must be parallel to each other, and a line perpendicular to one of two parallel lines is automatically perpendicular to the other (the Perpendicular Transversal Theorem). Expect a short proof, not just a fact to quote, along with algebra where the right-angle condition itself becomes an equation.

Examples

Example 1: Definition of perpendicular
If ℓ1⊥ℓ2\ell_1 \perp \ell_2ℓ1​⊥ℓ2​, what angle measure is formed, and how many angles have that measure?
Solution
Perpendicular lines mark a right angle with a small square, not an arc.
By definition, perpendicular lines intersect to form right angles — each exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Saying ℓ1⊥ℓ2\ell_1\perp\ell_2ℓ1​⊥ℓ2​ isn't just about one angle: it guarantees all four angles at the intersection are 90∘90^\circ90∘, not only the one drawn.
Answer: 90∘90^\circ90∘, and all four angles at the intersection equal it.
Example 2: Why all four angles must be right
Two lines are perpendicular. Explain, starting from just one 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle, why the other three angles at the intersection must also be 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Solution
Start from one right angle (90∘90^\circ90∘) at the intersection.
Its linear-pair partner is supplementary: 180∘−90∘=90∘180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ180∘−90∘=90∘ — also right.
Its vertical angle is congruent to it (Vertical Angles Theorem), so it is also 90∘90^\circ90∘; the fourth angle, vertical to the second, is 90∘90^\circ90∘ as well.
Answer: All four angles measure 90∘90^\circ90∘ — it only takes knowing one of them is right to force perpendicularity.
Example 3: Two perpendiculars to the same line
If ℓ1⊥t\ell_1 \perp tℓ1​⊥t and ℓ2⊥t\ell_2 \perp tℓ2​⊥t, what can you conclude about ℓ1\ell_1ℓ1​ and ℓ2\ell_2ℓ2​?
Solution
Lines a and b are both perpendicular to c — they never meet.
Both intersections with ttt create 90∘90^\circ90∘ angles in corresponding position (same side of ttt, same corner).
Since those corresponding angles are congruent (90∘=90∘90^\circ = 90^\circ90∘=90∘), the Converse of the Corresponding Angles Postulate applies.
Answer: ℓ1∥ℓ2\ell_1 \parallel \ell_2ℓ1​∥ℓ2​.
Example 4: Perpendicular Transversal Theorem
ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m and t⊥ℓt \perp \ellt⊥ℓ. What can you conclude about ttt and mmm?
Solution
t is perpendicular to ℓ; since ℓ ∥ m, it must be perpendicular to m too.
Perpendicular Transversal Theorem: if a line is perpendicular to one of two parallel lines, it is automatically perpendicular to the other.
Here t⊥ℓt\perp\ellt⊥ℓ and ℓ∥m\ell\parallel mℓ∥m satisfy exactly the theorem's hypothesis.
Answer: t⊥mt \perp mt⊥m.
Example 5: Any labeled angle is $90^\circ$
Two lines are perpendicular. One of the four angles at the intersection is labeled xxx. Find xxx.
Solution
It doesn't matter which of the four angles is labeled — perpendicularity forces every one of them to be a right angle.
Answer: x=90x = 90x=90.
Example 6: Linear pair with a right angle
One angle at an intersection is 90∘90^\circ90∘. Find the measure of the angle adjacent to it (its linear-pair partner).
Solution
Linear Pair Postulate: adjacent angles whose outer sides form a line are supplementary.
180∘−90∘=90∘180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ180∘−90∘=90∘.
Answer: 90∘90^\circ90∘ — confirming that a linear pair including one right angle always produces another right angle.
Example 7: Horizontal and vertical lines
On a coordinate plane, why are a horizontal line and a vertical line always perpendicular, even though the usual "slopes multiply to −1-1−1" rule doesn't directly apply?
Solution
The product rule (m1m2=−1m_1 m_2 = -1m1​m2​=−1) fails here because a vertical line's slope is undefined — you can't multiply by "undefined."
Instead, use the definition directly: a horizontal line makes a 0∘0^\circ0∘ angle with the xxx-axis, and a vertical line makes a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle with it, so the angle between the two lines themselves is 90∘90^\circ90∘.
Answer: Horizontal and vertical lines are always perpendicular — this is the one case handled by definition rather than the slope-product rule (see Section 2.4).
Example 8: Reading the right-angle mark
A diagram shows a small square drawn at an intersection instead of a curved arc. What does that convention mean, and why is it different from a general angle mark?
Solution
A curved arc marks a generic angle of unspecified measure; a small square specifically marks a 90∘90^\circ90∘ angle — it is a promise, not just a label.
Seeing the square lets you use 90∘90^\circ90∘ immediately in any equation, without being told the measure in words.
Answer: The square symbol means the angle is exactly 90∘90^\circ90∘ (the lines are perpendicular there).
Example 9: A short proof that two perpendiculars are parallel
Given: a⊥ca \perp ca⊥c and b⊥cb \perp cb⊥c. Prove: a∥ba \parallel ba∥b.
Solution
Both a and b meet c at 90°, in matching corresponding positions.
1. a⊥ca \perp ca⊥c and b⊥cb \perp cb⊥c — Given
2. The angle formed by a,ca,ca,c is 90∘90^\circ90∘; the corresponding angle formed by b,cb,cb,c is 90∘90^\circ90∘ — Definition of perpendicular
3. 90∘=90∘90^\circ = 90^\circ90∘=90∘, so the two corresponding angles are congruent — Substitution
4. a∥ba \parallel ba∥b — Converse of the Corresponding Angles Postulate
∎ Proved: a∥ba \parallel ba∥b.
Example 10: Algebra inside a right-angle condition
Transversal ttt meets line ℓ\ellℓ, forming an angle of (2x+10)∘(2x+10)^\circ(2x+10)∘ that is marked as a right angle. Given ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m, find xxx, and explain why ttt must also be perpendicular to mmm.
Solution
The right-angle mark tells us the expression itself equals 90∘90^\circ90∘: 2x+10=902x+10 = 902x+10=90.
2x=80⇒x=402x = 80 \Rightarrow x = 402x=80⇒x=40.
Since t⊥ℓt \perp \ellt⊥ℓ and ℓ∥m\ell \parallel mℓ∥m, the Perpendicular Transversal Theorem guarantees t⊥mt \perp mt⊥m as well.
Answer: x=40x = 40x=40; t⊥mt \perp mt⊥m.
