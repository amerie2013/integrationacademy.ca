3.5 Line and Rotational Symmetry
A figure has line symmetry if some reflection maps it onto itself, and rotational symmetry if some rotation less than 360∘360^\circ360∘ does the same — and these two properties are surprisingly independent, since a shape can have one without the other. This section explains why a regular nnn-gon always has exactly nnn lines of symmetry and rotational order nnn, using the Section 3.4 theorem that two reflections compose into a rotation, and it highlights the classic trap that a non-square rectangle's diagonals are not lines of symmetry at all.

Examples

Example 1: Equilateral triangle — three lines through vertex and opposite midpoint
How many lines of symmetry does an equilateral triangle have, and where exactly do they lie?
Solution
Each line runs from a vertex through the midpoint of the opposite side.
Each line of symmetry must map the triangle onto itself, which means it has to pass through one vertex and the midpoint of the side opposite that vertex (this keeps the two remaining vertices swapping places correctly).
Since there are 333 vertices, there are 333 such lines — one per vertex.
Answer: 333 lines of symmetry.
Example 2: Square — four lines, not just the diagonals
How many lines of symmetry does a square have, and what are they?
Solution
Two diagonals plus a horizontal and a vertical midline — four lines total.
A square's high symmetry comes from two different kinds of mirror lines: the 222 diagonals (which swap opposite vertices) and the 222 lines through the midpoints of opposite sides (horizontal and vertical).
2+2=42 + 2 = 42+2=4 lines total.
Answer: 444 lines of symmetry.
Example 3: Why a rectangle's diagonals are NOT lines of symmetry
A non-square rectangle has 222 lines of symmetry. Explain why the diagonals do not count, even though they do for a square.
Solution
Reflecting a rectangle over a diagonal would have to swap the two triangles the diagonal creates — but those triangles are only mirror images of each other when all four sides are equal (a square). In a non-square rectangle, the two legs of each triangle have different lengths, so the reflection does not land back on the original rectangle.
Only the line through the midpoints of the long sides, and the line through the midpoints of the short sides, actually work.
Answer: 222 lines of symmetry (through the midpoints of opposite sides) — the diagonals fail because the side lengths are unequal.
Example 4: Isosceles (non-equilateral) triangle
How many lines of symmetry does an isosceles triangle have if its three side lengths are not all equal?
Solution
Only the line through the apex (where the two equal sides meet) and the midpoint of the base works — it swaps the two equal sides onto each other.
Any other candidate line would have to match up sides of different lengths, which a reflection can never do (reflections preserve length).
Answer: 111 line of symmetry.
Example 5: Scalene triangle has none
Explain why a scalene triangle (all three sides different lengths) has zero lines of symmetry.
Solution
Any line of symmetry for a triangle must swap two of its sides, which requires those two sides to be equal in length (since reflections preserve length).
A scalene triangle has no two sides equal, so no such swap is possible.
Answer: 000 lines of symmetry.
Example 6: Connecting lines of symmetry to rotational order
A square has 444 lines of symmetry, each pair of adjacent lines meeting at 45∘45^\circ45∘. Using the Section 3.4 theorem that two reflections compose into a rotation of double the angle between the lines, find the square's smallest positive angle of rotational symmetry.
Solution
Section 3.4 theorem: reflecting over two intersecting lines equals a single rotation through twice the angle between them.
Two adjacent lines of symmetry meet at 45∘45^\circ45∘, so composing those two reflections gives a rotation of 2×45∘=90∘2\times45^\circ = 90^\circ2×45∘=90∘ — and since both reflections map the square onto itself, so must the resulting rotation.
Answer: 90∘90^\circ90∘, matching rotational symmetry of order 444 — in general, a shape with nnn evenly spaced lines of symmetry through one center automatically has rotational order nnn.
Example 7: Regular hexagon
Using the pattern from Example 6, state the number of lines of symmetry and the order of rotational symmetry for a regular hexagon.
Solution
A regular n-gon always has n lines of symmetry and rotational order n.
A regular nnn-gon always has nnn lines of symmetry and rotational order nnn (adjacent lines meet at 180∘n\dfrac{180^\circ}{n}n180∘​, so composing them gives a 360∘n\dfrac{360^\circ}{n}n360∘​ rotation).
For a hexagon, n=6n=6n=6.
Answer: 666 lines of symmetry; rotational symmetry of order 666 (smallest angle 60∘60^\circ60∘).
Example 8: Regular pentagon
A regular pentagon has 555 lines of symmetry. Find its smallest positive angle of rotational symmetry, and verify using 360∘÷order360^\circ \div \text{order}360∘÷order.
Solution
By the nnn-gon pattern, order =5=5=5 (matching the 555 lines of symmetry).
Smallest angle =360∘5=72∘= \dfrac{360^\circ}{5} = 72^\circ=5360∘​=72∘.
Answer: 72∘72^\circ72∘.
Example 9: Rotational symmetry without line symmetry
A parallelogram that is not a rectangle or a rhombus has 180∘180^\circ180∘ rotational symmetry about the intersection of its diagonals, but 000 lines of symmetry. Explain how both facts can be true at once.
Solution
A generic parallelogram: rotates onto itself, but no mirror line works.
Rotating 180∘180^\circ180∘ about the diagonals' intersection swaps each vertex with the opposite one, and opposite sides of a parallelogram are always equal and parallel — so the rotated figure lands back on the original.
No reflection works, though: a general parallelogram's slanted sides are not mirror images of each other across any line, since neither pair of sides is perpendicular to a potential mirror in the right way (unlike a rectangle or rhombus).
Answer: Rotational and line symmetry are independent properties — a shape can have one without the other.
Example 10: The circle — a limiting case
How many lines of symmetry does a circle have, and what is its rotational symmetry?
Solution
A circle looks identical from every direction, so any line through its center is a line of symmetry, and any rotation about its center (any angle at all) maps it onto itself.
This is the limiting case of a regular nnn-gon as n→∞n\to\inftyn→∞: infinitely many lines, and rotational symmetry with no smallest positive angle.
Answer: Infinitely many lines of symmetry; rotational symmetry for every angle.
