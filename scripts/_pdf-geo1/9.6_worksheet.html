9.6 Inscribed Triangles & Quadrilaterals
A polygon is cyclic when all of its vertices lie on a single circle. For a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite angles are always supplementary (180∘180^\circ180∘ total) — this follows directly from the Inscribed Angle Theorem, since each pair of opposite angles intercepts a pair of arcs that together make up the entire 360∘360^\circ360∘ circle. The converse is also true and useful: if a quadrilateral's opposite angles are supplementary, it can be inscribed in a circle. Thales' Theorem (from the previous lesson) is the triangle version of this idea: an inscribed triangle with one side as a diameter always has a right angle opposite that diameter. This worksheet combines both ideas, including the proof of the cyclic-quadrilateral rule and problems that mix arcs, angles, and algebra.

Examples

Example 1: Opposite angles are supplementary
Cyclic quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has m∠A=85∘m\angle A=85^\circm∠A=85∘. Find m∠Cm\angle Cm∠C.
Solution
Opposite angles AAA and CCC intercept arcs that together fill the whole circle.
Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180∘180^\circ180∘:
m∠C=180∘−85∘=95∘m\angle C=180^\circ-85^\circ=95^\circm∠C=180∘−85∘=95∘.
Answer: 95∘95^\circ95∘.

Example 3: Thales' Theorem in a cyclic figure
Triangle ABCABCABC is inscribed in a circle with diameter AC‾\overline{AC}AC. m∠A=40∘m\angle A=40^\circm∠A=40∘. Find m∠Cm\angle Cm∠C.
Solution
The angle opposite a diameter is always 90∘90^\circ90∘.
m∠B=90∘m\angle B=90^\circm∠B=90∘ (Thales' Theorem), so m∠C=180∘−90∘−40∘=50∘m\angle C=180^\circ-90^\circ-40^\circ=50^\circm∠C=180∘−90∘−40∘=50∘.
Answer: 50∘50^\circ50∘.
Example 4: Arcs and an inscribed angle
Cyclic quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD has arcs mAB^=80∘m\widehat{AB}=80^\circmAB=80∘, mBC^=95∘m\widehat{BC}=95^\circmBC=95∘, mCD^=xm\widehat{CD}=xmCD=x, mDA^=110∘m\widehat{DA}=110^\circmDA=110∘. Find xxx and m∠Bm\angle Bm∠B.

∠B\angle B∠B is inscribed and intercepts arc ADCADCADC (the arc from AAA to CCC through DDD, not containing BBB): mADC^=mAD^+mDC^=110∘+75∘=185∘m\widehat{ADC}=m\widehat{AD}+m\widehat{DC}=110^\circ+75^\circ=185^\circmADC=mAD+mDC=110∘+75∘=185∘.
m∠B=12(185∘)=92.5∘m\angle B=\dfrac{1}{2}(185^\circ)=92.5^\circm∠B=21​(185∘)=92.5∘.
Answer: x=75∘x=75^\circx=75∘; m∠B=92.5∘m\angle B=92.5^\circm∠B=92.5∘.
Example 5: Can it be cyclic?
A quadrilateral has m∠A=70∘m\angle A=70^\circm∠A=70∘ and m∠C=100∘m\angle C=100^\circm∠C=100∘ (opposite angles). Can it be inscribed in a circle?

Check the supplementary requirement: 70∘+100∘=170∘≠180∘70^\circ+100^\circ=170^\circ\neq180^\circ70∘+100∘=170∘=180∘.
Answer: No — the opposite angles don't sum to 180∘180^\circ180∘, so this quadrilateral cannot be inscribed in a circle.
Example 6: Opposite angles
A cyclic quadrilateral has m∠A=112∘m\angle A=112^\circm∠A=112∘. Find m∠Cm\angle Cm∠C.
Solution
Identify that ∠A\angle A∠A and ∠C\angle C∠C are opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral, so they must be supplementary.
Subtract from 180∘180^\circ180∘:
180∘−112∘=68∘180^\circ-112^\circ=68^\circ180∘−112∘=68∘.
Answer: 68∘68^\circ68∘.

Example 8: Thales — algebra
A right triangle is inscribed with the hypotenuse as a diameter; the other two angles are 3x3x3x and 2x2x2x. Find xxx and both angles.
Solution
3x+2x=90∘3x+2x=90^\circ3x+2x=90∘ (the two acute angles must complete the 90∘90^\circ90∘ right angle to reach 180∘180^\circ180∘ total):
5x=90∘⇒x=18∘5x=90^\circ \Rightarrow x=18^\circ5x=90∘⇒x=18∘.
Angles: 54∘54^\circ54∘ and 36∘36^\circ36∘.
Answer: x=18∘x=18^\circx=18∘; angles 54∘54^\circ54∘ and 36∘36^\circ36∘.
Example 9: Cyclic test
A quadrilateral has opposite angles 95∘95^\circ95∘ and 85∘85^\circ85∘. Can it be cyclic?
Solution
95∘+85∘=180∘95^\circ+85^\circ=180^\circ95∘+85∘=180∘.
Answer: Yes — the angles are supplementary, so the quadrilateral can be inscribed in a circle.
Example 10: Proving opposite angles are supplementary
Prove that opposite angles ∠A\angle A∠A and ∠C\angle C∠C of cyclic quadrilateral ABCDABCDABCD are supplementary.
Solution
∠A\angle A∠A is an inscribed angle intercepting arc BCDBCDBCD (the arc from BBB to DDD passing through CCC, which does not contain AAA): m∠A=12mBCD^m\angle A=\dfrac{1}{2}m\widehat{BCD}m∠A=21​mBCD.
∠C\angle C∠C is an inscribed angle intercepting arc BADBADBAD (the arc from BBB to DDD passing through AAA, which does not contain CCC): m∠C=12mBAD^m\angle C=\dfrac{1}{2}m\widehat{BAD}m∠C=21​mBAD.
Arcs BCDBCDBCD and BADBADBAD together make up the entire circle: mBCD^+mBAD^=360∘m\widehat{BCD}+m\widehat{BAD}=360^\circmBCD+mBAD=360∘.
Adding the two angle equations: m∠A+m∠C=12(mBCD^+mBAD^)=12(360∘)=180∘m\angle A+m\angle C=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(m\widehat{BCD}+m\widehat{BAD}\big)=\dfrac{1}{2}(360^\circ)=180^\circm∠A+m∠C=21​(mBCD+mBAD)=21​(360∘)=180∘.
The same argument (swapping which pair of arcs you use) proves ∠B\angle B∠B and ∠D\angle D∠D are supplementary too.
Example 11: Application — a circular skylight frame
A circular skylight is framed by quadrilateral PQRSPQRSPQRS inscribed in the circle. A carpenter cuts corner PPP at 118∘118^\circ118∘. What angle should the opposite corner RRR be cut at so the frame fits the circular opening?
Solution
Opposite corners of a cyclic quadrilateral must be supplementary:
m∠R=180∘−118∘=62∘m\angle R=180^\circ-118^\circ=62^\circm∠R=180∘−118∘=62∘.
Answer: 62∘62^\circ62∘.
